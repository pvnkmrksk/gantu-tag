Open, tiny Bluetooth LE tracking tags for insects that anyone can order assembled from JLCPCB, from about €2.30 a tag, then finish by hand.

Early pilots. The first panels arrived assembled from JLCPCB on 19 November 2026; the same evening, a Hagalu S6 was reported through Apple Find My three times in 20 minutes (±76–102 m). It advertises steadily from about 1.9 klx and weighs 0.242 g with its wire antenna. On 20 November two burned Hagalu tags, on light alone, were followed on a walk through town, one on Find My and one on Hubble (below). Other masses, lifetimes and costs are still design estimates.

Two tags, one panel

Both use the InPlay IN100 NanoBeacon, configured for Apple Find My (OpenHaystack static key) or Hubble when you burn its one-time-programmable memory. The hardware doesn't change between networks.

Field test: a walk through town

20 November 2026: two Hagalu tags, burned and running on light alone, carried through town. gantu_S6_01 on Apple Find My, Hagalu-002 on Hubble. Both were reported, including from inside a supermarket under ordinary shop lighting: the cluster of close points.

We used the Gantu dashboard to fetch and map both networks. Set it up on a Pi or laptop.

Approach

No GPS and no receiver on the tag: it only broadcasts a Bluetooth LE advert every few seconds. Phones and gateways on an existing network hear it and report where they were, and you fetch those reports.

One chip, configured

The InPlay IN100 NanoBeacon runs no firmware. You load a configuration over UART, test it in RAM, then burn it once into OTP. The tag is just the chip, a crystal and a few capacitors.

Apple Find My

Via OpenHaystack: the tag sends a fixed-key Find My advert, nearby iPhones relay its position to Apple, and you decrypt the reports with your private key. Free per tag.

Hubble Network

The IN100 is supported by Hubble: adverts are picked up by its terrestrial network of phones and gateways and, as its next satellites arrive, from orbit. Paid, with a free starter tier.

Built by JLCPCB

Every part is placed from JLCPCB's own stock on a 2-layer 0.6 mm board. You add only the antenna wire, the cell (Irulu) and epoxy.

Started by cutting

Programming pads sit on a tail. Like commercial bat and insect tags, you program, then cut the tail off; on Irulu V2 the cut is what switches it on.

Pilots first

The first panel carries several variants of each tag (S6/S6L/N6, V1/V2), so one order shows which start circuit works before anyone builds in bulk.

Build your own

No firmware to write and no fine-pitch soldering: JLCPCB places every part. The build guide has every step, the downloads, and a form that turns your key into a ready config.

Order

Upload the order files to JLCPCB: 0.6 mm, Panel by Customer, Standard PCBA, 2 assembled panels: 20 Hagalu and 16 Irulu tags.

Antenna

A 31 mm, 0.1 mm wire on the ANT pad, and a second on GND for a dipole.

Jig

Four jumper wires, four header pins and a rubber band. The pins wedge into the tail's 0.8 mm holes and hold by themselves.

Config

A ready-made config for Find My or Hubble. The only thing you add is your key.

Program

InPlay's free NanoBeacon Config Tool: test in RAM, then burn the one-time-programmable memory.

Finish & follow

Fit the cell on Irulu, cut the tail, coat in epoxy, and follow the tag through Find My or Hubble.

Roadmap

Lighter first, then simpler, then cheaper: cost is already low, while mass decides which insects can carry a tag. The full plan, with links to parts and suppliers, is in docs/roadmap.md.

Now · bench

Measure the v8.3 pilots: the mass split between board and parts, Irulu bring-up, the S6L and N6 start circuits, a smaller capacitor and range in the open. Try a superelastic nitinol antenna wire, which springs back instead of kinking, glued on with silver epoxy instead of solder: easier in bulk, and kind to Irulu's cell.

The IN100 in its 1.1 × 2.0 mm WLCSP package and 0201 parts. Irulu shrinks to a board no bigger than its cell.

Hagalu ≈ 0.05–0.07 g · Irulu ≈ 0.16–0.17 g with a 337

On Irulu the 0.13 g cell is the floor: the 337 is the smallest common silver-oxide cell and already outlasts a typical 1–3 week deployment. Targets are estimates until each stage is built and weighed.

About the name

Gantu (ಗಂಟು, gun-two, /ɡəɳʈu/) works on three levels.

A knot

The tag is tied onto the animal.

Clinging on

Colloquially, someone who is gantu to you sticks to you and goes wherever you go, as if knotted together. That's the tag and its insect.

A node

As on a plant stem or in a network. The tag is found as a node heard by a mesh of phones and gateways.
