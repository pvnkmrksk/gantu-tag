Build guide

From a JLCPCB order to a tag that reports where it's been. No firmware to write and no fine-pitch soldering: you fit a wire, load a configuration over four pins, and burn it.

Budget about €250–300 for the first order (36 tags) and €15–25 for tools you may not have. These are early pilots: check the latest notes before you spend money.

2Order the panels from JLCPCB

  1. Download the order zip and unzip it: a Gerber zip, the BOM and the placement (CPL) file.
  2. At jlcpcb.com, start an order and upload insect_tag_v8_combined_panel_gerbers.zip.
  3. Set the PCB options:

    Layers, material2, FR-4

    PCB qty5 (the minimum)

    Via coveringTented

  4. Turn on PCB Assembly: Standard (Economic only takes 0.8–1.6 mm boards), top side, 2 panels, tooling holes added by customer, confirm parts placement: yes.
  5. Upload insect_tag_v8_combined_panel_bom.csv as the BOM and insect_tag_v8_combined_panel_pos.csv as the CPL. Check stock: the IN100 (C6062024) and the TPS3831 (C2066667) were the tight ones.
  6. Check the placement preview before paying:
    • U1 (IN100): the pin-1 dot sits next to the crystal.
    • Y1 (crystal): a 90° rotation shorts it to GND and kills the tag; 180° is harmless.
    • U2 (TPS3831, Hagalu S tags only): pin-1 dot at the upper left. D2 (RB751): cathode bar towards C5.
    • Photodiodes: pad 1 is the cathode, marked by the silk bar; the middle row of each 2 × 3 block is turned 180°.
    • The bottom row of the panel is turned 180°, so its marks are too. Fix offsets in JLC's preview, not in the files.
  7. Pay. The first order took 13 days to arrive. You get 2 assembled panels (8 Hagalu S6, 8 S6L, 4 N6, 8 Irulu V1, 8 V2) and 3 bare ones. The variant is printed on each tag's underside.

Details and reasoning: order/README.md and panel/README.md.

3Break out a tag

  • Keep the tail on: it carries the four programming holes.
  • Don't snap the body out. Cut its tab with a flush cutter or scalpel from the panel side while supporting the tag, then sand the nub. C1 and U2 sit 0.2 mm from the long edges.
  • The tail's tabs are wide; snap or cut those however you like.

4Fit the antenna wire

One or two straight wires, each 31 mm: a quarter wave at 2.44 GHz.

  1. Cut 31 mm of ≈ 0.1 mm enamelled copper. Strip the last 1–2 mm (scrape it, or melt solderable enamel off with a hot iron) and tin it.
  2. Add flux to the ANT pad, lay the wire flat along it so it leaves over the edge, and reflow.
  3. For a dipole (recommended on Hagalu, and on Irulu for Hubble or long range): a second 31 mm wire on the GND pad, leaving the opposite way. Never join the two wires.
  4. Keep the joints low: Hagalu's underside faces the insect.

Which wire? Thin enamelled copper is light (≈ 2.2 mg per 31 mm at 0.1 mm, ≈ 8.7 mg at 0.2 mm) and easy to solder, but it kinks. Next on the roadmap: superelastic nitinol wire, which springs back straight, glued on with silver epoxy instead of solder. See the roadmap.

5Make the programming jig

Four jumper wires, four header pins and a rubber band. No pogo pins, no clamp, and it holds steady for the whole programming run.

The tail has four plated holes on a 2.54 mm grid, 0.8 mm drill. A square 0.64 mm header pin is ≈ 0.9 mm across its corners, so it wedges into the hole and holds by itself.

  1. Push a 2 × 2 block of male header pins into the female ends of four jumper wires. Two 2-pin pieces snapped off a strip work.
  2. Wrap a rubber band tightly round the four jumper housings so they can't splay. The pins now sit rigidly on the same grid as the holes.
  3. Wire the other ends to the adapter. The letters are printed next to each hole on the tail, and the square hole is G:
    TailAdapter
    G (square)GND
    +3V3 (never +5V)
    R (into the IN100)TXD
    T (out of the IN100)RXD
    G and T sit diagonally opposite each other, as do R and +. Leave DTR and +5V empty. If the tool can't find the chip, swap R and T: that's the usual mistake.
  4. Seat the jig in the tag first, then plug the adapter into USB. All four pins make contact before anything is powered, so the serial line can't back-power the chip. To power-cycle, unplug the USB, not the jig.

6Program the tag

The IN100 runs no firmware. A configuration (address, advert, interval, TX power, key) goes in over the serial port: first into RAM, where it's lost at power-off, then once into one-time-programmable memory.

Hagalu: program in dim light. Irulu: program before the cell goes on, and never connect the jig once a cell is fitted: it would drive 3.3 V into the cell.

6.2 Make the key and the config

The configs are 0 dBm, 1 Mbps, all three advertising channels, XO cap code 8; Find My every 3 s, Hubble every 4 s. They ran on real tags. This form only writes your key into them. It runs in your browser: the key isn't sent anywhere.

First register a device in the Hubble dashboard with encryption AES-128-EAX, counter mode DEVICE_UPTIME, period exponent 15. If these don't match, Hubble silently drops the packets.

Prefer the command line? python3 new_findmy_tag.py hagalu_01 or python3 new_hubble_tag.py hagalu_002 (downloads) write the same files, byte for byte, and keep a private record of each key. For Hubble you can also edit the template by hand: replace the 32 zeros in "key0" with your key in hex.

6.3 Test it in RAM

  1. Open the NanoBeacon Config Tool and Load your .cfg. Leave the advertising data format on Custom (the tool's own "Hubble" format sends the payload unencrypted).
  2. Seat the jig in the tag and plug the adapter in: a fresh power-up.
  3. Probe, pick the port (/dev/cu.usbserial-… on a Mac, COM… on Windows) and Connect straight away.
  4. Run in RAM. The tag starts advertising.
  5. Check it on the phone:
    • Find My: nRF Connect shows manufacturer data 0x004C with raw data starting 1EFF4C00121900, no flags. The form above shows the exact bytes and address to expect.
    • Hubble: service data for UUID 0xFCA6. Open the Hubble app near the tag. To check it yourself: python3 hubble_decode.py hagalu_002.hubble.key <service data hex> prints VCC, temperature and the advert count.

One RAM load per power-up. Once a configuration runs, the chip stops answering the tool: Connect fails, or Run in RAM reports Done and changes nothing. To load another one, Load it in the tool, unplug and replug the USB, Connect straight away, and Run in RAM once.

6.4 Burn it

When the RAM test works: power-cycle, Connect straight away, and Program (burn) instead of Run in RAM. This can be done only once per tag. Write down which key went on which tag. The tool keeps a copy of what it sent in its log folder (nano_beacon_config.cfg): compare it with your file.

Unplug: the tag now runs on its own. A Hagalu advertises only in light (steady from about 1.9 klx: a desk lamp at 20–30 cm, daylight, or a well-lit shop). An Irulu advertises once its cell is fitted.

7Finish the tag

Hagalu

  1. Cut the tail at CUT: score both faces with a scalpel and snap, or file through. Side cutters can smear VCC and GND together.
  2. Clean off the flux and seal the cut edge with a dot of epoxy.
  3. Coat the photodiodes thinly with clear, unpigmented epoxy. Keep all six equally lit: the most shaded one sets the current.
  4. Store tags in the dark.

Irulu

  1. V2 only: bridge the OFF jumper on the tail; the tag stays silent until you cut the tail.
  2. Optional: a Kapton washer (≈ Ø 7 mm, Ø 2 mm hole) on the underside, hole over the round − pad.
  3. A small dab of silver epoxy on the − pad; seat the cell − cap down, centred on the printed ring. Epoxy bridging cap and can shorts the cell.
  4. A short 0.2 mm wire from the + pad to the can's side, fixed with silver epoxy. Never solder to the cell. 8331D cures in ≈ 6 h at room temperature or 10 min at 65 °C.
  5. Check: + and G on the tail read the cell voltage. V1 now advertises; V2 is silent.
  6. V2 in the field: cut the tail (scalpel, not side cutters: the cell is live) and seal the edge.
  7. Encapsulate in epoxy, leaving the antenna free beyond the first millimetre.

8Follow it

Self-host the Gantu dashboard on a Pi or laptop to collect both networks, map each tag's history, and read the Hubble sensor payload.

Find My

Nearby iPhones report the tag's position to Apple, encrypted to your key. Fetch and decrypt the reports with the private key from your .keys file, using an OpenHaystack-compatible fetcher such as FindMy.py or macless-haystack (Docker; it runs on a Raspberry Pi). Both need an Apple ID.

Hubble

Packets show up in the Hubble app and dashboard. Hubble's packets API and webhooks return each packet with the relay's location and the payload already decrypted, as base64; parse_sensors() in hubble_decode.py turns it into VCC, temperature and advert count.

Payload

Byte 0: VCC × 0.03125 V. Bytes 1–2: temperature, signed, × 0.01 °C. Bytes 3–6: advert count. All little-endian. Mh0LAAAAAA== is 1.562 V, 28.45 °C, count 0.

What it looked like

A walk on 20 Nov 2026 with two Hagalu tags running on light alone: one on Find My, one on Hubble. The cluster of close points is inside a supermarket, under ordinary shop lighting.

On light alone, every Hubble packet checked read 1.562 V and advert count 0: at that supply the counters don't advance, so on Hagalu they carry no information, and the rotating ID stays at its first value (Hubble still decodes it). VCC and temperature are still useful.
